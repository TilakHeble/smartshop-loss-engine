08 · Fairness of the policy as deployed, and sizing a store trial¶

Two questions before the business case:

  1. Is any group of honest shoppers stopped unfairly often under the recommended policy, as it would actually run (shoppers respond, trust updates over four cycles, 10 runs)?
  2. How many stores would a randomised trial need to confirm the headline in real stores?

Both are computed by python -m smartshop.reporting.snapshot; this notebook reads its outputs. Demographics are joined only here, never in a model or the policy.

1. Honest-shopper stop rates¶

The adverse outcome is being asked for a full rescan when honest. For every attribute: the four-fifths ratio (lowest group rate / highest), an interval that resamples households, the ratio if every group had the same basket sizes, and a permutation test: the ratio random groups of the same sizes would show. With up to 11 groups of as few as 11 households, the lowest/highest ratio sits well below 1 by chance alone, so the raw ratio on its own over-reports unfairness.

Reading it.

  • Channel, new SmartShop users, evening peak: pass (ratios 0.96–1.0). The week 5 fixes hold once trust and shopper responses are included; trust does not disadvantage new users, because without history they simply get the standard rate.
  • Income: the gap is within chance. Random groups this small give a ratio of about 0.61; the observed 0.60 is no worse.
  • Household size: a real gap, explained by basket size. Larger households buy bigger baskets, and bigger baskets carry more risk for everyone; at equal basket sizes the ratio is 0.81.
  • Age: a real gap that basket size only partly explains (0.61, or 0.76 at equal basket sizes; random groups would give about 0.80). The drivers are basket-level features: the basket model's expected missing value, basket value and number of products. Younger households (age groups 2–3) buy the largest baskets.

In absolute terms no group is worse off than today: the most-stopped group in any attribute is stopped at 31–45% of today's rate. The remaining review item is relative: whether a business is content that younger family households are stopped about 1.6x as often as the oldest when honest, because their baskets are bigger. That is a policy decision for the business, and the trial below is where it would be monitored (Nectar's consented demographic data, aggregated).

2. Sizing a store-level randomised trial¶

The simulator cannot prove the headline for real stores. A trial can: randomise stores (matched in pairs on baseline loss rate) to today's random rescans or the recommended policy at the same colleague time, and compare each store's loss rate over the trial period, adjusted for its own baseline period (CUPED / ANCOVA).

Measuring loss in both arms the same way is the point of week 8: each arm keeps random full rescans (today's 2% random full rescans; the policy's 2% floor), and each store's loss is estimated from them. The error of that estimate is not assumed: the random floor gave a 15% error over the whole test period (47,314 sessions, 30 runs, as in week 8), and it scales with one over the square root of sessions. For a store with 20,000 SmartShop sessions in the trial period that is about 23%, which is why measurement noise, not only the spread between stores, drives the trial size.

The spread of loss rates between real stores, and how stable a store's loss rate is from one period to the next (rho), cannot be estimated from simulated stores (too few households each), so the table shows a range. Stores per arm to detect the 12% headline with 80% power at 5% significance:
