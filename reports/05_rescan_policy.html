05 · The rescan game: a policy that holds up when shoppers adapt¶

The risk model says who looks risky. The policy decides who gets a full rescan, with what probability, using the same colleague time as today. It has to work against shoppers who learn it: a policy that only works while thieves are unaware of it does not work.

The game (Stackelberg). The store leads: it commits to a policy, a function from risk score to the probability of a full rescan, which shoppers learn over time. Shoppers follow:

  • opportunists steal on impulse, less often the likelier a rescan
  • adaptive shoppers choose how much to take (0, 25, 50 or 100% of their usual haul) to maximise haul × (1 − q·d) − q·d·penalty. Taking less keeps the scan log calmer and the risk score lower: the way to slip under a threshold
  • everyone else is unaffected (honest and careless shoppers do not steal; deliberate ones are not deterred)

For every session, each version of reality (honest, and each haul size) is simulated once from the same random draws, with its own scan log, risk score, loss and rescan findings. Evaluating a policy is then exact: work out each shopper's best choice under it and add up.

Rules. Every policy uses today's colleague time. Policy shapes are fitted on the tuning period (days 331–500); the test period (501–711) only evaluates, with rescan volume set to its budget, as a store sets volume to its staffing each week. Every policy keeps a 2% random floor of full rescans: the unbiased measurement and training labels from weeks 3 and 5.

Simplification. Member history is held at today's; the game is played one session at a time.

4. Why evaluate against shoppers who respond¶

The same threshold policy, judged two ways: against shoppers who keep behaving as they do today (what a static evaluation assumes), and against shoppers who learn the policy.

5. Does randomising beat a threshold?¶

Game theory says a deterministic threshold can be exploited, and it is: under the threshold about a fifth of adaptive sessions hold back to stay under it. But randomising recovers little of that here. To deter an adaptive shopper, the rescan chance they face must exceed haul / (d·(penalty + haul)); the table shows how high that is for the hauls they actually plan, against the chance a policy can afford to give them.

Deterring a typical adaptive shopper would need a rescan chance of about a third, far beyond what the budget allows (about 7% of sessions get a full rescan). So the store cannot buy deterrence from them with randomisation; it wins by detection, catching thieves whose scan logs give them away. That makes the best policy here close to a threshold with a random floor; randomising mainly earns its place through the cap (section 6). If thieves were more cautious (a higher perceived penalty), deterrence would matter more: week 8 tests that.

Today's many cheap partial rescans do deter: under random full rescans at the same cost (fewer rescans), more opportunists steal (22% vs 18%). The recommended policy accepts a little less deterrence for about 5x the recovery.

6. Fairness: who gets stopped, and how often¶

In [9]:
demo = con.sql('SELECT * FROM staging.stg_member_demographics').df()

def fairness(policy):
    ch = responses(g_test, policy)
    p = policy(pick('score', ch))
    honest = pick('deliberate_value', ch) == 0
    df = pd.DataFrame({'member_id': s.member_id, 'p': p, 'honest': honest})
    out = {}
    d2 = df.merge(demo, on='member_id', how='left')
    for col in ['age_group', 'household_size', 'income_level']:
        dd = d2[d2.honest].dropna(subset=[col])
        r = dd.groupby(col).agg(rate=('p', 'mean'), n=('p', 'size'))
        r = r[r.n >= 200]
        out[f'honest-stop ratio: {col}'] = r.rate.min() / r.rate.max()
    m = df.groupby('member_id').p.mean()
    out['highest member rescan rate'] = m.max()
    out['members rescanned on >50% of trips'] = int((m > 0.5).sum())
    return out

rows = {}
for name, params in fitted.items():

    rows[name] = {'loss remaining': outcome(g_test, pol)['loss_remaining'], **fairness(pol)}

Random selection is fair by construction but recovers little. Every risk-based policy stops honest shoppers far less often overall, but not equally across groups: households with bigger baskets are stopped more (week 5). The cap's clear effect is on individuals: with a cap of 0.5 no member is rescanned on more than half their trips on average (without a cap, some honest members would be rescanned on nearly every trip). Its effect on group gaps is small and mixed (better for age and income, worse for household size, within the noise of groups with very few households). Recommended: cap 0.5, for about 3% more loss than an uncapped threshold. The group gaps are not solved by the policy; they are a monitored review item (week 10) and a reason the colleague, not the score, makes the call.

Findings¶

# Finding Implication
1 Same colleague time as today: the recommended policy leaves 12% less loss, recovers 5.4x as much ($11 to $57 per colleague hour) and stops 69% fewer honest shoppers Rescans go where they find things
2 Judged against shoppers who keep today's behaviour, the threshold policy's recovery is overstated by 37% Policies must be evaluated against shoppers who learn them
3 Adaptive shoppers do exploit the threshold (about a fifth hold back), but randomising gains little: deterring them needs about a one-in-three rescan chance, and the budget allows about 7% on average Here the store wins by detection, not deterrence; the policy is close to threshold + floor
4 Today's cheap partial rescans deter some opportunists; fewer, targeted full rescans deter less but find far more A deliberate trade, quantified
5 Risk-based policies stop honest shoppers unequally across age, household size and income; a cap of 0.5 ensures no one is rescanned on more than half their trips, but moves group gaps only a little, in both directions Cap 0.5 recommended; group gaps stay a monitored review item (week 10)
6 The 2% random floor stays in every policy Unbiased loss measurement and training labels (weeks 3 and 5)

Assumptions to stress-test (week 8): the adaptive shopper's penalty and perceived detection, the opportunists' sensitivity to rescans, and weaker risk signals.
